Title:  Genre 2.0? Embedding-Based Cluster Analysis as a
                    Tool for Text Classification in Medieval Hebrew Literature
Volume:  019
Issue:  3
Date:  19 September 2025
Abstract:  
                
                The classification of medieval Hebrew literature has long relied on historically
                    inherited genre labels, often leading to misassignments and blurred textual
                    boundaries. This study applies transformer-based cluster analysis to a corpus of
                    60 texts, using BEREL embeddings and hierarchical clustering to evaluate whether
                    selected computational methods can provide empirically grounded, complementary
                    insights to traditional genre classifications. The analysis identifies several
                    stable clusters, including a distinct Narrative cluster, reinforcing
                    prior research that questions the categorization of certain texts as Aggadic
                        Midrash. While some clusters align with established classifications,
                    others highlight ambiguities that challenge conventional taxonomies. The study
                    demonstrates that computational clustering can systematically capture textual
                    affinities, revealing relationships that may remain obscured in traditional
                    approaches. These findings establish a methodological framework for reassessing
                    genre structures in Hebrew literature, laying the groundwork for future research
                    based on expanded datasets and manuscript evidence.

                1. Introduction
                Medieval Hebrew narrative literature has remained largely underrepresented in the
                    study of Hebrew literary history. In contrast, fields such as Hebrew poetry and
                    religious texts have traditionally occupied a more central position in Jewish
                    Studies (cf. ; . One
                    consequence of this imbalance is the continued reliance on foundational
                    scholarship from the Wissenschaft des Judentums, the
                    19th- and early 20th-century academic movement that shaped the categorization of
                    Hebrew texts (cf. ). While these early studies were
                    instrumental in establishing the field, they also introduced classificatory
                    frameworks that remain in use despite their limitations. In particular, medieval
                    Hebrew literary genres lack precise definitions, as their categorization
                    continues to reflect historical terminologies rather than clear formal or
                    stylistic distinctions. As  has demonstrated, the
                    term Midrash has often been applied indiscriminately to medieval texts
                    that do not align with the genre’s original definition. This overextension of
                    the Midrash label has obscured the distinctiveness of Hebrew narrative
                    texts, relegating them to the margins of literary scholarship and limiting their
                    systematic study.
                Rather than advancing a renewed genre theory, this study takes an empirical
                    approach, asking: Do Hebrew narrative texts, rather than being subsumed under
                    the broad category of Midrash, form a distinct and coherent cluster when
                    analyzed through quantitative methods? If so, this would indicate that
                    computational approaches can detect textual affinities and distinctions that
                    have been overlooked by traditional classifications. To explore this, the study
                    applies embedding-based cluster analysis to a dataset of 60 medieval Hebrew
                    texts, grouping them according to patterns captured by a transformer model
                    rather than relying on predefined genre labels. The aim is to test whether
                    computational methods can reveal textual affinities that align with or challenge
                    existing scholarly categories. The findings lay the groundwork for further
                    research that will refine the dataset and methodology, particularly through the
                    inclusion of manuscript-based texts.
                Transformer models like BEREL 2.0 are deep learning architectures trained on
                    large corpora to recognize contextual patterns in language. Instead of relying
                    on manually defined features, they generate embeddings — dense numerical
                    representations that capture semantic, syntactic, and structural properties of a
                    text. Each text in this study is represented by a vector summarizing its
                    internal linguistic behavior. BEREL 2.0, trained specifically on historical
                    Hebrew, enables the comparison of these vectors, allowing texts to be grouped
                    not by external genre labels but by patterns in how they use language.
                The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows: The next section provides an
                    overview of previous computational approaches to Hebrew text analysis, tracing
                    the development of stylometric methods, clustering techniques, and the more
                    recent use of transformer-based embeddings in Jewish literary studies. This is
                    followed by a description of the dataset and methodology, including text
                    selection, preprocessing, and clustering procedures. The results section
                    presents the key findings, highlighting the emerging clusters and their
                    implications for existing classifications. Finally, the discussion considers
                    both the potential and limitations of computational methods, and the conclusion
                    outlines directions for future research.

                    previous research, adapting them to a new textual corpus and a different
                    literary-historical context. By combining BERT-based embeddings with
                    hierarchical clustering, it explores whether this approach can offer a
                    data-driven complement to traditional genre classification.
            

                3. Data and Methodology
                
                    3.1. Corpus Selection
                    The dataset for this study consists of 60 Hebrew texts from antique,
                        rabbinic, and medieval literary traditions, including a few modern Hebrew
                        translations of earlier works originally preserved in other languages. The
                        majority of these texts were obtained through the Git repository of the
                        Sefaria website (Link to GitHub). Sefaria, a widely used online library of Jewish texts,
                        categorizes its collection into the following groups: Tanakh,
                        Mishnah,
                        Talmud,
                        Kabbalah,
                        Liturgy,
                        Jewish Thought,
                        Tosefta,
                        Chasidut,
                        Musar,
                        Responsa, and Second Temple. These categories reflect a
                        conventional classification of Jewish texts and serve as a point of
                        reference in this study, allowing for a systematic comparison between
                        traditional categorizations and the data-driven clusters that emerge through
                        computational analysis.
                    To ensure that the cluster analysis would produce distinct and meaningful
                        clusters, five texts were selected from each of the following categories:
                            Tanakh (= the Hebrew Bible), Talmud,
                        Aggadic Midrash,
                        Halakhic Midrash,
                        Halakha,
                        Liturgy,
                        Jewish Thought,
                        Musar, and Second Temple. The selection was made largely at
                        random, with the only curating criterion being technical constraints, such
                        as a minimum text length of 20,000 characters (though the shortest included
                        text contains approximately 28,000 characters). This process resulted in a
                        core dataset of 45 texts.
                    In addition, 12 further texts from Sefaria’s Aggadic Midrash category
                        were included, each displaying varying degrees of narrative characteristics.
                        These texts have been previously discussed in scholarly debates concerning
                        their classification, making them particularly relevant for assessing how
                        computational methods distinguish between Midrashic and narrative literature
                        (e.g., ; ; ; ; ). Their inclusion allows for a closer examination
                        of whether narrative tendencies influence their clustering and to what
                        extent they align with or diverge from traditionally defined Midrashim.
                        Furthermore, three additional texts were incorporated into the dataset based
                        on previous research indicating their narrative character. Since they are
                        not available on Sefaria — and thus do not fall within its categorization —
                        these texts were obtained from freely accessible online sources such as
                        Wikitext. 
                    Two texts from the previously mentioned category Aggadic Midrash, Petirat Moshe and Petirat
                            Aharon, were transcribed directly from manuscript witnesses rather
                        than drawn from later printed sources. Most other texts in the dataset are
                        based on later textual traditions, including printed editions, later
                        recensions, and editorially standardized versions. The planned move toward
                        manuscript-based sources is discussed further in section 5.2. Limitations
                            & Future Work.
                    The 60 texts included in this study were preprocessed before analysis to
                        ensure consistency and eliminate non-textual clues that could interfere with
                        clustering. Special characters, annotations, and nikud (vowel markings), when present, were systematically removed;
                        in addition, embedded biblical quotations were excluded as part of the
                        preprocessing. Following this, texts exceeding 100,000 characters were
                        truncated to that length, with the selected segment taken from the middle of
                        the text to avoid biases introduced by introductory or concluding
                        sections.
                
                
                    3.2. Text Representation
                    To generate numerical representations of the texts, this study employed BEREL
                        2.0, a transformer model specifically trained on rabbinical Hebrew texts
                            . BEREL is based on the BERT
                        (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) architecture,
                        which enables it to capture deep contextual and syntactic relationships
                        within texts . BEREL 2.0 was selected as the
                        sole embedding model for this experiment, as it has been found to be the
                        most suitable for processing medieval Hebrew literature (cf. ). While future research may include comparative
                        evaluations of other transformer models, BEREL 2.0 proved to be effective
                        for the present task.
                    Each text was tokenized into 512-token windows with a stride of 256, ensuring
                        overlapping segments that preserved contextual continuity. The CLS token
                        representation was extracted from each window, as this embedding encodes a
                        summary of the segment’s linguistic characteristics. To obtain a single
                        representation per text, the CLS embeddings from all windows were averaged,
                        creating a 768-dimensional vector that captured the overall semantic,
                        syntactic, and structural properties of the text. This approach ensured that
                        longer texts were not disproportionately influenced by their opening or
                        closing sections, instead providing a balanced representation of the full
                        document.
                    Since BERT-based embeddings are inherently high-dimensional, a two-step
                        dimensionality reduction process was applied to facilitate clustering and
                        visualization. The original 768-dimensional embeddings contain both
                        meaningful linguistic patterns and noise, making direct clustering
                        computationally inefficient. To retain the most relevant features, Principal
                        Component Analysis (PCA) first reduced the embeddings to 50 dimensions,
                        filtering out less informative variance while preserving key structural
                        patterns. To enable clustering, Uniform Manifold Approximation and
                        Projection (UMAP) was applied, reducing the data to two dimensions. UMAP was
                        chosen over alternative methods such as t-SNE due to its ability to maintain
                        both local and global relationships between texts. 
                    This study clusters directly in UMAP 2D for computational feasibility and
                        interpretability. Although this necessarily discards some variance, UMAP is
                        designed to preserve meaningful structural relationships even in low
                        dimensions. To mitigate potential instability, UMAP was run 50 times, and
                        the final projection was averaged across runs, preventing random variations
                        from distorting the clusters.
                    Given the effectiveness of this approach, as shown in the 4. Results
                        section, there was no immediate need to explore clustering in
                        higher-dimensional spaces for this proof of concept. However, future studies
                        may benefit from testing clustering in higher-dimensional UMAP spaces (e.g.,
                        10D or 20D) to evaluate whether additional variance further refines
                        classification results. For a discussion on the potential limitations of
                        dimensionality reduction, see 5.2. Limitations & Future Work.
                    
                        3.3. Clustering Approach
                        To identify meaningful textual groupings, this study applied hierarchical
                            clustering to the UMAP-reduced embeddings. Clustering, in this context,
                            refers to the process of grouping texts based on numerical
                            representations that capture internal patterns of language use and
                            structure. The more similar two texts are, the closer their
                            corresponding vectors are in the multidimensional space, which results
                            in them being merged earlier in the hierarchical clustering process.
                            Unlike methods such as K-means, hierarchical clustering does not require
                            specifying the number of clusters in advance, making it well-suited for
                            an exploratory study of textual relationships. Ward’s method was chosen,
                            as it minimizes intra-cluster variance, ensuring that texts within a
                            cluster are as similar to each other as possible. Instead of setting a
                            fixed number of clusters, the threshold for merging clusters was
                            dynamically adjusted to produce a meaningful number of clusters, ranging
                            between eight and ten.
                        To illustrate the clustering process, Figure 1 presents a UMAP projection
                            based on a single clustering iteration. This visualization shows how
                            texts are distributed in the two-dimensional space generated by UMAP,
                            providing a simplified view of their relative positions in the
                            high-dimensional embedding space. The projection offers an intuitive
                            representation of the textual relationships captured in one iteration of
                            the pipeline.

                        To ensure the robustness of the clustering results, the entire pipeline,
                            including embedding generation, was repeated 100 times. Since
                            transformer-based models like BEREL 2.0 produce slightly different
                            embeddings in each run, individual cluster assignments showed minor
                            variations across iterations. Rather than relying on a single clustering
                            outcome, a co-occurrence matrix was constructed, tracking how often each
                            text appeared in the same cluster across all 100 runs.
                        A second round of hierarchical clustering was then applied to this
                            matrix, identifying stable groupings that consistently emerged across
                            runs. These stable clusters — referred to in this article as
                            meta-clusters — reflect recurrent textual affinities independent of
                            run-to-run variation. Their distribution is visualized in a
                            co-occurrence heatmap (Figure 2), which highlights the strength of
                            associations between texts across the 100 iterations.
                        In addition to storing cluster memberships, the pipeline also recorded
                            the pairwise distance between each pair of texts in every run. These
                            distance values, derived from the position of each text in the reduced
                            embedding space, represent the degree of dissimilarity between texts:
                            the lower the value, the more similar the texts. By averaging these
                            distances across all runs, a new distance matrix was created that
                            reflects the overall proximity of texts throughout the experiment.
                        A second clustering solution was then derived by applying Ward’s
                            hierarchical clustering directly to the averaged distance matrix. Unlike
                            the meta-clusters, which reflect co-clustering frequency, this second
                            approach sorts texts based on cumulative pairwise dissimilarity. The
                            result is a dendrogram showing hierarchical relationships among all
                            texts, with cluster boundaries generated by an automated distance
                            threshold (Figure 3).
                        Both the meta-clusters derived from co-occurrence patterns and the
                            distance-based clusters obtained from averaged pairwise dissimilarities
                            resulted in ten groupings. While the two structures broadly align,
                            subtle differences highlight texts that occupy boundary positions
                            between clusters — cases that are addressed in section 4.
                                Results.
                        
                    
                
                
                    3.4. Evaluation & Validation
                    The co-occurrence matrix confirms that clusters formed consistently,
                        indicating that the results are not random but reflect meaningful textual
                        similarities. The emerging clusters align closely with expected
                        classifications, reinforcing confidence in the method. The heatmap in Figure
                        2 demonstrates that texts with known affinities are repeatedly grouped
                        together across iterations. A complementary clustering structure, derived
                        from the averaged distance measurements between texts, yielded a similar
                        grouping of the corpus, further supporting the robustness of the approach.
                        While factors such as text length and embedding variations were considered
                        potential biases, their influence appears minimal. A more detailed
                        evaluation of methodological limitations follows in section 5.
                            Discussion.
                
            

                4. Results
                
                    4.1. General Overview
                    Both clustering approaches applied in this study — meta-clustering based on
                        co-occurrence patterns and hierarchical clustering of averaged distance
                        measurements — converged on a shared structure of ten distinct clusters. To
                        preserve interpretability and analytical coherence, the number of clusters
                        was constrained to a range between eight and ten; within this range, both
                        methods independently arrived at a division into ten groups. This clustering
                        structure is echoed in both visualizations (Figure 2 and Figure 3),
                        highlighting how texts consistently formed meaningful groupings across
                        runs.
                    Notably, these clusters do not always align with Sefaria’s predefined
                        categories. Rather than relying on external (genre) classifications, they
                        are derived from internal textual patterns — semantic, syntactic, and

                    To maintain clarity and transparency throughout this research paper, each
                        text is named according to a consistent convention. The name begins with its
                        traditional classification as listed in Sefaria, followed by a period, and
                        then the individual text title (e.g., Midrash Aggada. Shemot Rabba).
                        This structure ensures that the original genre assignment remains visible,
                        allowing for direct comparison between traditional categorizations and the
                        clusters identified in this study. 
                    The cluster names used in this study were assigned post hoc based on the
                        composition of each group, aiming to provide a neutral and descriptive
                        representation of the texts contained within them. Whenever possible, the
                        labels were aligned with traditional literary classifications, ensuring that
                        they reflect recognizable textual traditions rather than imposing new genre
                        definitions.
                    This is particularly relevant when considering the assignment of the term
                            Narrative to one of the clusters, which includes several texts
                        traditionally classified as Aggadic Midrash. In previous research
                            
                        
                        , it has been observed that certain texts labeled
                        as Midrash exhibit a distinctly narrative structure while lacking the
                        formal characteristics of midrashic literature, raising questions about
                        their classification. However, defining clear criteria for distinguishing
                        narrative texts from Midrashim has remained a challenge. 
                    The cluster analysis presented here offers an empirical perspective on this
                        issue, as one of the clusters emerged with a distinctive stylistic profile
                        aligning with features typically associated with narrative texts. This
                        grouping was not imposed in advance and only recognized retrospectively
                        based on its clear separation from other clusters and its shared textual
                        characteristics. By relying on measurable textual similarities, rather than
                        preconceived genre definitions, this approach provides a data-driven means
                        of reassessing textual classifications. The naming conventions used
                        throughout this research paper serve as interpretative tools, describing the
                        observed clustering patterns without enforcing rigid genre distinctions.

                    4.3. Key Findings
                    The clustering results reveal stable and meaningful groupings, though these
                        do not always align with Sefaria’s predefined categories. Rather than
                        replicating traditional genre classifications, the clusters reflect patterns
                        in textual structure, language use, and composition, allowing texts to be
                        grouped based on internal features rather than external labels. This
                        highlights how embedding-based similarities can transcend historical or
                        thematic classifications, resulting in clusters that are broader and more
                        flexible than conventional genre categories.
                    The following discussion of cluster contents proceeds in the order of
                        appearance in Table 1, which lists both the meta-cluster and distance-based
                        cluster assignments. 
                    The first cluster may be described using the label Midrash Aggada. In
                        the distance-based clustering, this group is relatively cohesive, comprising
                        six core texts: Bereshit Rabba, Shemot Rabba, Bamidbar
                            Rabba, Devarim Rabba, Tanhuma Buber, and Aggadat
                            Bereshit. The meta-clustering approach includes two additional
                        works: Pirke deRabbi Eliezer and Mekhilta deRabbi Yishmael. The latter, however,
                        already belongs to a more mixed grouping of midrashic and narrative works in
                        the distance-based clustering.
                    Despite these minor differences in cluster boundaries, both approaches
                        confirm the internal stylistic coherence of the core aggadic midrashim.
                        Their textual similarities outweigh distinctions in historical context or
                        thematic content, supporting the conclusion that their classification as
                            Aggadic Midrashim is not merely conventional but grounded in
                        measurable linguistic and structural features. Notably, the included texts
                        span from approximately the 4th to the 12th centuries, yet they are
                        consistently grouped together by the model. This suggests that the features
                        captured by BEREL, such as lexical choice, syntactic structure, and
                        discursive patterns, reflect a unique stylistic cluster profile that
                        persists across centuries, and therefore includes both earlier and later
                        Midrashim alike.
                    The next group can be described as Midrash/Narrative Mix. It appears
                        in both clustering outcomes, albeit with slight differences in text
                        allocation, as previously discussed. In both cases, the works in this
                        cluster are positioned between the Midrash Aggada and the
                            Narrative groups, suggesting that they share features with both.
                        Most of the included texts are traditionally classified as Midrash
                            Aggada, though Sifre Zuta stands out as
                        an exception, having been traditionally identified as a halakhic midrash.
                        The cluster analysis indicates that all of these works exhibit at least
                        partial narrative characteristics, positioning them stylistically between
                        the more homogeneous Midrash Aggada and Narrative clusters. 
                    The third group, described as the Narrative cluster, is identical in
                        both clustering approaches. It consists of six works and is closely related
                        to a fourth cluster that also contains texts with predominantly narrative
                        character. The separation of these two groups results from the threshold
                        applied during the clustering process: Had the cutoff been set slightly
                        higher, they would have merged into a single, broader cluster.
                    The emergence of this distinct Narrative cluster is a central finding of the
                        study. This grouping includes texts that are traditionally labeled as
                            Aggadic Midrashim, not only in Sefaria’s classification but also
                        in broader scholarly discourse. Some even bear the word Midrash in their titles, such as Midrash
                            Aseret ha-Dibberot (The Midrash of the Ten
                            Commandments). Despite these labels, the clustering consistently
                        identified their stronger affinity with other works characterized by
                        narrative structure, suggesting that their stylistic and linguistic features
                        align more closely with narrative compositions than with midrashic works
                        from the Midrash Aggada group. The clustering process, grounded in
                        internal textual features rather than inherited genre designations,
                        highlights these affinities and challenges the conventional classification
                        schemes that have long shaped the field.
                    The next cluster, broadly labeled Philosophy, appears identically in
                        both clustering outcomes. It brings together texts that Sefaria
                        distinguishes as either Musar or Jewish Thought, but which the
                        clustering process grouped into a single coherent category. From a
                        computational perspective, this suggests that the boundary between these two
                        designations may be less rigid than traditionally assumed.
                    A related cluster contains just three texts, Sefer
                            Mitzvot Gadol, Kol Bo, and Sefer haYira, and also appears identically in both
                        clustering outcomes. Conventionally, these works are classified as either
                        halakhic (Sefer Mitzvot Gadol, Kol Bo) or ethical (Sefer
                            haYira). In the meta-clustering, they show partial overlap with
                        both the Talmud/Halakha and Philosophy clusters, while in the
                        distance-based clustering, they are positioned closer to the broader
                            Philosophy group. Nonetheless, these three works demonstrate a
                        consistent stylistic affinity with one another that sets them apart from
                        their neighboring clusters. Their internal cohesion was strong enough for
                        both clustering methods to isolate them as a distinct group. For the
                        purposes of this study, this cluster is labeled Halakha–Philosophy
                            Mix, reflecting the dual influence of legal and
                        ethical-philosophical traditions within these texts.
                    A group of works broadly categorized as Bible/Pseudo-Bible appears in
                        both clustering outcomes, though with minor internal differences. The
                        overall cluster consists of ten texts that are either part of the biblical
                        canon or imitate its style. Both clustering methods separate the group into
                        two smaller subclusters but do so in different ways.
                    In the distance-based clustering, three works, Sefer
                            Makkabim I, Sefer Makkabim II, and the
                        midrashic Sefer haYashar, are set apart as a
                        stylistically coherent subgroup. These texts of different backgrounds
                        imitate the language and structure of the biblical narrative, marking them
                        as pseudo-biblical compositions. In contrast, the meta-clustering
                        approach isolates three canonical books, Leviticus,
                            Deuteronomy, and Isaiah,
                        as a separate cluster, while grouping Genesis and Joshua together with pseudo-biblical texts. 
                    Both clustering approaches yield results that are interpretable: Either the
                        canonical books (with the exception of Genesis and
                            Joshua) form a separate group, or the
                        pseudo-biblical works of later origin cluster together. The placement of Genesis and Joshua alongside
                        biblical imitations reflects their influence as stylistic models, while the
                        grouping of later pseudo-biblical compositions underscores their shared
                        narrative form. Although it is difficult to determine exactly which features
                        led to the separation in each case, the resulting groupings align well with
                        known patterns and raise no unexpected contradictions.
                    The Liturgy cluster is likewise identical in both clustering outcomes
                        and forms a cohesive group overall. Within the cluster, however, the
                        visualizations reveal two internally distinct subgroups. One consists of the
                        prayer books — texts labeled as Siddur or Machzor — while the other includes works
                        traditionally classified as Second Temple. Although these two
                        subgroups originate from different historical contexts and were composed for
                        different purposes, their proximity in the embedding space suggests a level
                        of formal or discursive similarity. The transformer-based model recognizes
                        consistent textual features across both types — perhaps in terms of
                        formulaic structure, elevated diction, or liturgical framing — which brings
                        them into alignment despite their thematic and functional differences. The
                        clustering thus reflects both their shared stylistic core and their internal
                        differentiation.
                    The final cluster, labeled Halakha, is the most internally unified
                        group in the dataset and appears identically in both clustering outcomes.
                        The distinctive linguistic and structural features characteristic of
                        talmudic-halakhic discourse set them apart from all other works included in
                        the study. Their strong affiliation, detected across both the co-occurrence
                        and distance-based analyses, reflects and reinforces traditional genre
                        attributions. The halakhic texts form a vector-space signature, further
                        validating the clustering approach used in this study.
                
                
                    4.4. Notable Observations and Interpretive Implications
                    Atypical placements within the clustering process can offer valuable entry
                        points for deeper textual analysis. Whether through unexpected affinities or
                        hybrid textual profiles, such results highlight irregularities that may hold
                        particular relevance for scholars working on the individual texts. More
                        broadly, the analysis demonstrates that computational clustering can uncover
                        patterns of literary behavior that transcend conventional genre categories,
                        inviting both methodological reflection and new avenues of literary
                        interpretation.
                    One such case is the placement of Midrash Tannaim.
                        While this work consistently aligns with halakhic texts in the
                        distance-based clustering, the meta-clustering reveals an interesting
                        pattern: across several iterations, Midrash Tannaim
                        also clustered with liturgical texts. This positioning suggests the presence
                        of stylistic features that link it not only to halakhic discourse but also
                        to formal or structural traits found in liturgical compositions. Given that
                        parts of this work are later reconstructions, the observed behavior may
                        reflect influences from the sources used in this process, offering valuable
                        clues for scholars investigating the text’s editorial history and
                        compositional layers.
                    Another example of interpretive interest is the work Mishle
                            Sendebar. As a Hebrew version of a widely circulated medieval
                        frame-tale narrative, it exists in multiple cultural and linguistic
                        traditions. Mishle Sendebar clusters consistently
                        with the Narrative group in both clustering outcomes. The
                        co-occurrence heatmap, however, reveals a weaker secondary affinity with the
                            Philosophy cluster. This outcome reflects a certain stylistic
                        hybridity, likely connected to the text’s Arabic Vorlage.
                    A third case that invites closer attention is the clustering of Petirat Moshe and Petirat
                            Aharon. The analysis shows that these texts do not clearly align
                        with the Midrash Aggada cluster, as one might expect based on
                        traditional classification. This outcome reflects recent scholarship that
                        places them in a more narrative framework .
                        Rather than forcing a definitive categorization, the embedding-based
                        analysis identifies stylistic affinities with both narrative and midrashic
                        texts. This dual positioning offers a compelling, data-driven starting point
                        for integrating traditional genre assignments with more recent interpretive
                        developments, demonstrating the method’s potential to mediate between
                        competing classifications.
                    The scattering of texts traditionally grouped under Midrash Halakha
                        across several clusters further underscores the model’s sensitivity to
                        internal textual variation. While some of these texts align with the
                            Halakha group, others are distributed across the Narrative
                        and Midrash/Narrative Mix clusters. This dispersion may be partly
                        influenced by technical factors such as text truncation (see 5.2.
                            Limitations & Future Work), but in many cases, only detailed
                        case studies will uncover the textual or structural features that shaped
                        these results. Their varied placement should be seen not as anomalies, but
                        as productive starting points for future research.
                    A last pattern to be pointed out is the clustering of texts composed in a
                        pseudo-biblical style from different historical periods. It supports the
                        idea that biblical imitation functions as a stylistic marker independent of
                        historical or canonical context. This reinforces the view that so-called
                            Rewritten Bible traditions can be empirically identified through
                        computational analysis, and that rewriting is best understood as an
                        ongoing literary method rather than a discrete genre category (cf. ).
                    This article does not aim to offer a comprehensive interpretation of each
                        individual observation derived from the presented clustering results.
                        Rather, it serves as a proof of concept for embedding-based cluster analysis
                        in the study of Hebrew literature. The findings presented here demonstrate
                        that the method can reveal meaningful textual relationships and
                        genre-blurring tendencies — details that merit closer examination in future
                        research.
                
            

                5. Discussion
                
                    5.1. Implications
                    One of the challenges in applying embedding-based methods to literary
                        classification is the opacity of machine-generated clusters. Unlike
                        traditional literary categories, which are explicitly defined based on
                        historical, thematic, or formal criteria, computational clusters emerge from
                        statistical patterns in textual features — patterns that remain largely
                        uninterpretable from a data science perspective. Coherent and meaningful
                        clusters emerge, but the model’s rationale for linking specific texts cannot
                        always be fully explained, as it draws on intricate and largely opaque
                        similarity structures in the embedding space.
                    From a humanistic perspective, this ambiguity does not diminish the value of
                        the results but instead offers two distinct paths for further research.
                        First, scholars can attempt to interpret the clusters post hoc, identifying
                        the underlying stylistic, rhetorical, or structural features that justify
                        their formation. For example, what linguistic elements unify Rewritten
                            Bible texts across different historical periods? Second, these
                        clusters can serve as a starting point for broader literary and cultural
                        inquiries. Scholars might, e.g., compare how specific themes, such as the
                        Death of Moses, manifest in texts within the Narrative cluster versus
                        those in the Midrash Aggada cluster. By grounding such studies in
                        empirically derived groupings rather than inherited genre labels, we
                        increase the likelihood of identifying meaningful textual relationships.
                    The results of this study demonstrate that embedding-based cluster analysis
                        is not merely a means of validating existing classifications; it actively
                        challenges and refines them. The clusters identified here do not simply
                        replicate conventional genre labels but instead reveal measurable textual
                        affinities, highlighting both expected relationships and areas where
                        traditional classifications obscure significant textual connections.
                    One of the most striking findings is the emergence of a distinct cluster
                        that, based on its composition, has been retrospectively labeled
                            Narrative. The cluster itself formed automatically; its label was
                        only assigned post-analysis, reinforcing the argument that certain texts
                        traditionally classified as Aggadic Midrash are, in fact, better
                        understood as independent narrative compositions. 
                    It should be mentioned that this does not mean narrativity is confined to
                        this cluster. Many texts outside of it, such as those categorized as
                            Bible/Pseudo-Bible,
                        Talmud/Halakha, and Midrash Aggada, contain strong narrative
                        elements yet do not cluster with the Narrative group. The texts in
                        the Narrative cluster are so stylistically dominated by narrative
                        structure that they form a computationally detectable unit, separate from
                        texts that incorporate narrative elements within a broader literary
                        framework. 
                    By analyzing texts based on measurable intrinsic similarities rather than
                        inherited classifications, this study reveals new relationships between
                        works that may have been overlooked due to historical misclassifications.
                        Traditional taxonomies have often grouped texts together based on
                        assumptions about their function, theme, or historical context, whereas
                        embedding-based clustering highlights affinities rooted in linguistic form,
                        structural organization, and deeper representational patterns. As a result,
                        certain connections have remained obscured, while other groupings may have
                        been artificially imposed. The findings here demonstrate how computational
                        methods can help correct and refine these historical classifications,
                        allowing us to identify clusters that more accurately reflect textual
                        composition.
                    Importantly, embedding-based clustering does not replace traditional literary
                        scholarship — rather, it enhances it by providing a systematic and empirical
                        basis for text classification. The results confirm that computational
                        methods can reliably distinguish well-defined textual traditions, while also
                        highlighting ambiguous cases that demand further literary and philological
                        analysis. By extending these methods, future research can explore broader
                        questions of intertextuality, manuscript transmission, and literary
                        influence within Hebrew literature.
                
                
                    5.2. Limitations & Future Work
                    The results of this study provide meaningful insights into the classification
                        of medieval Hebrew texts, although several limitations must be considered
                        when interpreting the findings.
                    First, the textual sources used in this analysis present certain constraints.
                        The corpus consists of online-available versions, which often reflect later
                        textual traditions rather than manuscript-based readings. Manuscript sources
                        are generally preferable, as they preserve earlier textual layers and
                        minimize the influence of editorial reshaping found in printed editions.
                        Future research should aim to work directly with manuscript evidence, which
                        would allow for a more precise understanding of textual features without the
                        influence of later edited text versions. New transcriptions of handwritten
                        Hebrew sources are expected to become available soon through the ERC-funded
                        project MiDRASH (https://doi.org/10.3030/101071829). Comparative experience with
                        other medieval corpora may also offer useful points of reference (cf. ). The fact that two texts, Petirat Moshe and Petirat Aharon, were
                        transcribed from manuscript sources rather than from later recensions had no
                        known impact on the presented results. An additional constraint lies in the
                        fact that, although all texts were cleaned and standardized, the corpus as a
                        whole was not manually checked for editorial inconsistencies.
                    Second, the size of the corpus imposes certain limitations. While 60 texts
                        provide a preliminary overview, they do not allow for fine-grained
                        distinctions between subcategories. This limitation becomes evident in cases
                        such as Tanhuma Buber, which clustered with
                            Midrash Aggada due to the absence of other texts from the Tanhuma-Yelammedenu genre in the dataset (cf. ; ). Likely, a
                        more extensive dataset would enable clearer distinctions between only
                        loosely related texts or cases like the Halakha/Philosophy Mix
                        micro-cluster. Furthermore, corpus selection was constrained by
                        availability. Texts were chosen not only based on scholarly relevance but
                        also on accessibility, meaning that some works were omitted simply due to a
                        lack of reliable online versions.
                    In addition, the corpus contains a relative imbalance, as the Midrash
                        category is more strongly represented than others. This reflects the central
                        role of the Midrash–narrative distinction in the present study. The results
                        indicate that this imbalance did not distort the clustering: Smaller but
                        more distinct categories, such as Talmud,
                        Liturgy, and Philosophy, still formed stable groups, while the
                            Midrashic–narrative spectrum displayed internal differentiations
                        that align with philological expectations. This suggests that the embeddings
                        captured genuine textual similarities rather than simply reproducing prior
                        group sizes. As a proof of concept, the present study provides a foundation
                        for further research, and its results strongly encourage follow-up studies
                        including broader and more balanced corpora.
                    A further limitation concerns text truncation. To ensure uniformity, all
                        texts were analyzed as 100,000-character samples, always taken from the
                        middle section of each text. On the one hand, this approach provides a
                        consistent basis for comparison; on the other hand, it may have affected the
                        classification of longer texts. For example, the work Sifra was assigned to the Narrative cluster. It is possible
                        that selecting a different text segment would have resulted in stronger
                        alignment with the Talmud/Halakha group. This suggests that
                        truncation may have influenced the clustering results in some cases. Future
                        studies should explore the impact of different segmentation strategies.
                    Additionally, the dataset includes texts of varying lengths, ranging from
                        28,000 to 100,000 characters. No strong correlation between text length and
                        clustering behavior was observed, but the possibility of length-based biases
                        cannot be entirely ruled out. A more controlled dataset — where text length
                        is systematically balanced — could help determine whether this factor has
                        any impact on clustering results.
                    When interpreting the findings of this study, the potential effect of
                        dimensionality reduction on clustering accuracy must also be taken into
                        account. While the two-step reduction (PCA to 50D, UMAP to 2D) effectively
                        structured the data for insightful preliminary observations, clustering
                        directly in 2D may have discarded some variance. This choice prioritized
                        interpretability and computational feasibility. The results demonstrate that
                        stable, meaningful clusters emerged despite this reduction. Future studies,
                        however, may explore clustering in higher-dimensional spaces (e.g., 10D or
                        20D) to assess whether additional variance improves classification
                        accuracy.
                    Another consideration is the choice of BEREL embeddings. No alternative
                        models were tested for comparison. The decision to use BEREL 2.0 was based
                        on prior research indicating its effectiveness for handling pre-modern
                        Hebrew texts, and the results of this study confirm that it produced
                        meaningful and expected clusters. Future research could still explore
                        whether other transformer-based models, potentially fine-tuned on medieval
                        Hebrew corpora, yield similar or even improved results. Testing multiple
                        embeddings could help determine whether stylistic clustering outcomes are
                        model-dependent or if they remain stable across different representations of
                        textual features.
                    A further methodological limitation concerns the interpretability of
                        embedding-based clustering. Since transformer models encode texts as dense
                        vectors without an explicit decoder, the rationale for grouping particular
                        texts cannot be directly recovered. In this respect, the method applied here
                        produces clusters whose internal logic must be analyzed post hoc through
                        philological and literary analysis, which provides a humanistic framework
                        for interpretation rather than an explanation of the encoding process
                        itself. Future research may seek to complement this approach with more
                        fine-grained examinations on the digital side, for instance, by combining
                        embedding-based clustering with selected stylometric features, or by
                        experimenting with segment-level representations rather than aggregated text
                        embeddings. Such strategies will not render the embedding process
                        transparent, but they may provide additional interpretive traction and help
                        clarify whether the observed affinities hold consistently across different
                        portions of a text.
                    Despite the outlined limitations, the overall clustering patterns align well
                        with expected textual relationships, confirming the robustness of the
                        presented method. 
                
            

                6. Conclusion
                This study has demonstrated that embedding-based clustering is an effective tool
                    for identifying textual affinities in Hebrew literature. The results confirm
                    that computational methods can both validate traditional classifications and
                    highlight areas where inherited taxonomies require refinement. The emergence of
                    a distinct Narrative cluster supports the argument to reconsider some
                    historically inherited genre labels in medieval Hebrew literature, suggesting
                    that certain texts traditionally categorized as Aggadic Midrash may be
                    more accurately described as independent narrative compositions under
                    contemporary classification frameworks. At the same time, clusters such as
                        Liturgy,
                    Talmud/Halakha, or Bible/Pseudo-Bible proved to be highly stable,
                    reinforcing established textual groupings. Other clusters, such as
                        Midrash/Narrative Mix, exhibit a more fluid internal structure, which
                    provides valuable insights into texts that occupy intermediate positions between
                    literary traditions.
                Beyond its immediate findings, this study demonstrates that computational
                    approaches provide a powerful, data-driven means of identifying textual
                    relationships. Categorization remains an essential scholarly tool — allowing for
                    meaningful comparisons, distinctions, and analyses of parallel developments.
                    Without classification, it would be impossible to trace literary dependencies,
                    influences, and thematic connections across texts. However, within the study of
                    medieval Jewish literature, existing classifications have often lacked
                    precision, relying on overly broad terminology that has not proven analytically
                    useful. Instead of simply redefining literary genres, this study argues that a
                    parallel approach is now possible — one that, in addition to traditional
                    reasoning, empirically derives textual relationships from semantic, structural,
                    and rhetorical patterns. This is a methodological enhancement that has only
                    recently become feasible, as advances in computational analysis allow for
                    objective, data-driven classification systems. 
                A valuable outcome of this study is that the clustering methodology and
                    computational pipeline developed here can be easily reapplied to larger and more
                    refined corpora. As more manuscript-based texts become available, the same code
                    and algorithm can be used to test whether the same stable clusters emerge in a
                    more comprehensive dataset. This ensures that the approach remains scalable and
                    adaptable, allowing for continued refinement of textual classifications without
                    requiring significant modifications to the underlying methodology.
                Although these findings are preliminary, they provide a solid foundation for
                    further research. Future studies should expand the dataset, incorporate
                    manuscript-based texts, and refine clustering methodologies. Furthermore, the
                    effectiveness of BEREL 2.0 embeddings in this experiment suggests that additional
                    comparative tests with other models could further enhance the reliability of
                    transformer-based classifications. While immediate follow-up research will
                    likely focus on refining the computational approach, the present study has
                    already yielded meaningful results on the level of literary analysis. The
                    observed cluster structures not only validate the method but also point to
                    interpretive insights that merit deeper engagement within Jewish literary
                    studies. A more detailed investigation into medieval Hebrew narrative literature
                    is already planned as part of the upcoming Digital Humanities postdoctoral
                    project Dynamics in the Polysystem: A Study of Hebrew
                        Narrative Prose and its Changing Status within the Jewish Literary Canon of
                        the Middle Ages, which will be conducted at the University of Haifa
                    from November 2025 onward, funded as a Walter Benjamin Fellowship by the DFG
                    (German Research Association).
